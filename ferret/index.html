Monitoring Your Ferret's Breathing and Heart Health

Cardiology for Animals · David Pelio, DVM, DACVIM (Cardiology)

A rise in your pet's resting breathing rate can be an early sign of heart failure, often before anything else changes. Exotic pets hide illness well, so the breathing count, appetite, droppings and body weight are the best tools you have at home.

How to countBreath counterWhat to watch for

How to count breaths

  • Wait until your pet is asleep or resting quietly. Do not count right after handling, eating, play or a car ride.
  • Watch the chest or body wall. One rise and one fall is one breath.
  • Count for 30 seconds and double it, or count for a full minute.
  • Write the number down with the date and time.

Learn your pet's normal first

For the first week, count once a day at about the same time and place. That week gives your pet's own baseline. For most exotic species no home breathing-rate cutoff has been published, so we compare your pet with itself. After the first week, count as often as your cardiologist recommends, and any time the breathing looks different.

If the number is high

Recount a few times over the next hour or two while your pet is calm. If it stays above your pet's usual range, or above the number your cardiologist gave you, call your veterinarian the same day. If a high rate comes with any sign in the emergency list below, go to an emergency hospital that sees your species.

Ferret

Heart disease in ferrets shows up as tiredness, less play, weak back legs, faster or harder breathing, coughing and a swollen belly (Schoemaker & van Zeeland 2021). No home breathing-rate cutoff has been published for ferrets, so the first week of counts sets your ferret's normal.

Every day

  • Resting or sleeping breathing rate and effort.
  • Energy and play, appetite, and how well the back legs work.
  • Weigh once a week.

Call your veterinarian the same day if

  • The resting rate stays above your ferret's usual range.
  • Coughing, sleeping more, or less interest in play.
  • The belly looks swollen, or weight changes.

Go to an emergency hospital now if

  • Labored or open-mouth breathing.
  • Collapse or sudden weakness of the back legs. In ferrets this can also be low blood sugar from an insulinoma, which needs treatment right away (Schoemaker & van Zeeland 2021).
  • Fainting, or not eating at all.
Report paragraph (copy for charts) 
Recommend the owner monitor [Patient]'s resting or sleeping respiratory rate and effort daily, read against the ferret's own baseline recorded over the first week (no ferret threshold has been published), with body weight weekly. If any signs are appreciated, such as a resting respiratory rate persistently above [Patient]'s baseline, labored breathing, coughing, lethargy, hind-limb weakness, collapse, abdominal distension or reduced appetite, the owner should take [Patient] to be evaluated by [Patient]'s exotic animal veterinarian or the closest emergency facility that sees ferrets for after-hours care; a blood glucose is measured in any weak or collapsing ferret (Schoemaker & van Zeeland 2021).

No home breathing-rate cutoff has been published for ferrets, so reading the rate against your ferret's own first-week baseline is this practice's position; your cardiologist may set a different number for your ferret.

Sources: Schoemaker NJ, van Zeeland YRA. Noninfectious diseases of ferrets. MSD Veterinary Manual, professional version; 2021. · Counting method adapted from the Cardiac Education Group respiratory-rate handout, 2015.
