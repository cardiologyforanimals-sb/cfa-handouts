Monitoring Your Rabbit's Breathing and Heart Health

Cardiology for Animals · David Pelio, DVM, DACVIM (Cardiology)

A rise in your pet's resting breathing rate can be an early sign of heart failure, often before anything else changes. Exotic pets hide illness well, so the breathing count, appetite, droppings and body weight are the best tools you have at home.

How to countBreath counterWhat to watch for

How to count breaths

  • Wait until your pet is asleep or resting quietly. Do not count right after handling, eating, play or a car ride.
  • Watch the chest or body wall. One rise and one fall is one breath.
  • Count for 30 seconds and double it, or count for a full minute.
  • Write the number down with the date and time.

Learn your pet's normal first

For the first week, count once a day at about the same time and place. That week gives your pet's own baseline. For most exotic species no home breathing-rate cutoff has been published, so we compare your pet with itself. After the first week, count as often as your cardiologist recommends, and any time the breathing looks different.

If the number is high

Recount a few times over the next hour or two while your pet is calm. If it stays above your pet's usual range, or above the number your cardiologist gave you, call your veterinarian the same day. If a high rate comes with any sign in the emergency list below, go to an emergency hospital that sees your species.

Rabbit

A healthy rabbit at rest breathes about 30 to 60 times a minute, and stress can push that much higher (Orcutt & Malakoff 2020). Rabbits hide heart disease until it is advanced: faster or harder breathing and a fast heart are typical, and coughing is uncommon (Orcutt & Malakoff 2020).

Every day

  • Resting breathing rate and effort.
  • Appetite and the number and size of droppings. Some heart medicines, especially diuretics, can reduce appetite.
  • Weigh once a week on a kitchen scale, same time of day.

Call your veterinarian the same day if

  • The resting rate stays above your rabbit's usual range.
  • Your rabbit eats less or passes fewer or smaller droppings.
  • Weight drops, or the belly looks swollen.
  • Less activity, or reluctance to move.
  • The eyes begin to bulge (this can come from a mass in the chest).

Go to an emergency hospital now if

  • Open-mouth breathing. Rabbits breathe through the nose, so this is always serious.
  • Hard, labored breathing, or bluish gums or tongue.
  • Collapse, or not eating at all.
Report paragraph (copy for charts) 
Recommend the owner monitor [Patient]'s resting or sleeping respiratory rate and effort, appetite and fecal output daily, and body weight weekly, using the rabbit's own baseline recorded over the first week; normal resting respiratory rate in rabbits is approximately 30–60 breaths per minute (Orcutt & Malakoff 2020). If any signs are appreciated, such as a resting respiratory rate persistently above [Patient]'s baseline, open-mouth or labored breathing, reduced appetite or fecal output, weight loss, abdominal distension, lethargy, bulging eyes or collapse, the owner should take [Patient] to be evaluated by [Patient]'s exotic animal veterinarian or the closest emergency facility that sees rabbits for after-hours care.

Reading the breathing rate against your rabbit's own first-week baseline is this practice's position; your cardiologist may set a different number for your rabbit.
