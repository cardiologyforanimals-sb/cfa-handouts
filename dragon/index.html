Monitoring Your Bearded Dragon's Breathing and Heart Health

Cardiology for Animals · David Pelio, DVM, DACVIM (Cardiology)

A rise in your pet's resting breathing rate can be an early sign of heart failure, often before anything else changes. Exotic pets hide illness well, so the breathing count, appetite, droppings and body weight are the best tools you have at home.

How to countBreath counterWhat to watch for

How to count breaths

  • Wait until your pet is asleep or resting quietly. Do not count right after handling, eating, play or a car ride.
  • Watch the chest or body wall. One rise and one fall is one breath.
  • Count for 30 seconds and double it, or count for a full minute.
  • Write the number down with the date and time.

Learn your pet's normal first

For the first week, count once a day at about the same time and place. That week gives your pet's own baseline. For most exotic species no home breathing-rate cutoff has been published, so we compare your pet with itself. After the first week, count as often as your cardiologist recommends, and any time the breathing looks different.

If the number is high

Recount a few times over the next hour or two while your pet is calm. If it stays above your pet's usual range, or above the number your cardiologist gave you, call your veterinarian the same day. If a high rate comes with any sign in the emergency list below, go to an emergency hospital that sees your species.

Bearded dragon

Heart disease in bearded dragons is quiet. In a study of 54 dragons with heart and blood-vessel disease, the common signs were a change in alertness or behavior, a smaller appetite, weight loss, regurgitation, darkening of the throat, and breathing effort, and most cases were found only after death (Ozawa et al 2024). Regular weighing and watching for these changes are the best early warning.

Count at the same temperature every time

A dragon's heart and breathing rates rise and fall with body temperature. Count at the same point in the day, for example after the same time under the basking lamp, so the numbers can be compared. A count without the same warming is hard to interpret.

Every week

  • Weigh on a gram scale.
  • Note appetite, alertness and basking behavior.
  • Count resting breaths at your set time.

Call your reptile veterinarian the same day if

  • Your dragon is duller than usual or stops basking normally.
  • Appetite drops, weight falls, or food comes back up.
  • The throat or beard stays dark.

Go to an emergency hospital that sees reptiles now if

  • Open-mouth or labored breathing.
  • A swollen body, or a firm swelling you can see or feel.
  • Weakness or collapse.
Report paragraph (copy for charts) 
Recommend the owner weigh [Patient] on a gram scale weekly and record appetite, alertness, basking behavior and a resting respiratory rate counted at the same time of day and after the same basking period, because heart and respiratory rates vary with body temperature. If any signs are appreciated, such as dullness or altered behavior, reduced appetite, weight loss, regurgitation, persistent darkening of the beard, open-mouth or labored breathing, coelomic distension or weakness, the owner should take [Patient] to be evaluated by [Patient]'s reptile veterinarian or the closest emergency facility that sees reptiles for after-hours care (Ozawa et al 2024).

No home breathing-rate cutoff has been published for bearded dragons; counting at a fixed time after basking and reading against your dragon's own baseline is this practice's position.

Sources: Ozawa SM, Pierce KV, Alexander AB, et al. Cardiovascular disease in central bearded dragons (Pogona vitticeps): 54 cases (2007–2022). Am J Vet Res 2024;85(5). · Counting method adapted from the Cardiac Education Group respiratory-rate handout, 2015.
